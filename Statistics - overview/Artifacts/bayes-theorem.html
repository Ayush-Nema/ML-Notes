Intuition: changing your mind by the right amount

Bayes' theorem is a rule for updating a belief when evidence arrives. You start with a belief (the prior), you see something, and you end with a new belief (the posterior). Two things decide how far you move: how much more likely the evidence is if you are right than if you are wrong, and how believable your starting point was.

That is why a strong test cannot rescue a very unlikely hypothesis, and a weak one cannot dislodge a near-certain one.

The theorem exists because we usually know the wrong direction. Test makers can measure how the test behaves on people whose status is known, which gives P(positive | sick). You hold a result and want P(sick | positive). Bayes' theorem converts one into the other, and the conversion needs one extra ingredient: how common the condition was to begin with.

Where the formula comes from

It takes four short steps from the definition of conditional probability (see the previous file). Let A be "has the condition" and B be "tests positive".

  1. The overlap can be written in two orders:
    P(A ∩ B) = P(A | B) P(B)
    P(A ∩ B) = P(B | A) P(A)
  2. Both equal the same overlap, so set them equal:
    P(A | B) P(B) = P(B | A) P(A)
  3. Divide both sides by P(B). This is Bayes' theorem:
    P(A | B) = P(B | A) P(A)P(B)
  4. You rarely know P(B) directly, so expand it with the law of total probability. A positive result comes either from a sick person or from a healthy one:
    P(A | B) = P(B | A) P(A)P(B | A) P(A) + P(B | Ac) P(Ac)

In the explorer above, the top of that fraction is the red group in the tree (true positives) and the bottom is the red group plus the blue group (all positives). That is Step 2 in a single line.

The four pieces

  • Prior, P(A). What you believed before seeing the result. Here it is the base rate: how common the condition is among the people being tested. It can also be a judgment call when no clean statistic exists.
  • Likelihood, P(B | A). How well the hypothesis predicts the evidence. Here, how often sick people test positive. It is not the probability of the hypothesis, and the likelihoods of different hypotheses do not have to add up to 1.
  • Evidence, P(B). How common this result is overall, sick and healthy people together. A result that is common even among healthy people is weak evidence.
  • Posterior, P(A | B). Your updated belief. Today's posterior becomes tomorrow's prior.

The odds form: evidence is a multiplier

Odds are another way to write a probability: odds = p ÷ (1 − p), and back again, p = odds ÷ (1 + odds). A 1% chance is odds of 1 to 99. Write Bayes' theorem for "sick" and for "not sick", divide one by the other, and the awkward P(B) cancels:

P(A | B)P(Ac | B) = P(A)P(Ac) × P(B | A)P(B | Ac)    i.e.   posterior odds = prior odds × likelihood ratio

The likelihood ratio says how many times more likely this result is for a sick person than for a healthy one. It belongs to the test alone, so a positive result is worth the same multiplier for everyone. What differs between people is where they start.

Repeated independent tests simply multiply again, which is what Step 4 showed: each positive result multiplies the odds by the same factor. A multiplier of 18 turns a tiny prior into a modest one, and a large prior into near certainty.

More than two hypotheses

The denominator generalizes by adding one term per hypothesis. For hypotheses H1, …, Hn that cover every possibility without overlapping, and evidence E:

P(Hi | E) = P(E | Hi) P(Hi)Σj P(E | Hj) P(Hj)

Example. A company has three factories. A, B and C make 50%, 30% and 20% of its phones, and their defect rates are 1%, 2% and 5%. One phone turns out to be defective. Which factory is it most likely from?

Mistakes and limits

  • Base rate neglect. Treating "the test is 90% sensitive" as "a positive means a 90% chance". It ignores the prior, which is exactly the number the theorem says you cannot skip.
  • The prosecutor's fallacy. "A match this good happens by chance 1 time in 10,000, so there is a 1 in 10,000 chance the defendant is innocent." That swaps P(evidence | innocent) for P(innocent | evidence). The right answer also depends on how many people could have matched.
  • Priors are inputs, not facts. When the base rate comes from good data, the posterior is solid. When it is a guess, the posterior is only as good as the guess, and reasonable people may start from different priors. With enough strong evidence, different starting points tend to converge.
  • Independence is an assumption. Step 4 multiplied odds as if each test's errors were unrelated. If a person is a false positive because of something about their body, retesting with the same method may repeat the error.
  • Error rates are not always fixed. Real sensitivity and false positive rates can differ between groups and conditions. Using one pair of numbers for everyone is a simplification.

What to take away

A test's accuracy and the chance you have the condition are different numbers. Bayes' theorem connects them through the starting rate: P(sick | positive) = P(positive | sick) × P(sick) ÷ P(positive).
