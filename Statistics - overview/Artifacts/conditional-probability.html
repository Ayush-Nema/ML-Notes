Intuition: probability is area, and conditioning is zooming in

Picture the whole shop as a square with area 1. The probability of an event is the share of the square it covers. If someone tells you "this customer bought a coffee", they are telling you the customer is somewhere inside the coffee column. Everything outside it is now impossible, so you throw it away and stretch what remains until it has area 1 again. That stretch is the division in the formula below.

Column width is the share of customers in each group. The coloured block inside a column is the share of that group who bought a pastry. Use the buttons to zoom in on a group.

The definition

For events A and B with P(B) > 0, the probability of A given B is the overlap divided by the size of B:

P(A | B) = P(A ∩ B)P(B)

The symbol ∩ means "and": both events happen. The bar | means "given" or "restricted to the cases where".

This is the same thing as counting dots. If n is the number of customers, then P(A ∩ B) ÷ P(B) = (nAB ÷ n) ÷ (nB ÷ n) = nAB ÷ nB. The total cancels, which is why "count the overlap, divide by the group you know about" always works.

The condition P(B) > 0 matters: you cannot restrict to a group that has nobody in it.

The multiplication rule

Rearrange the definition and you get a rule for "both happen". To get both, first the one event must happen, then the other must happen within it:

P(A ∩ B) = P(A | B) × P(B)
P(A ∩ B) = P(B | A) × P(A)

Both lines describe the same overlap, just reached in a different order. For three events the rule chains: P(A ∩ B ∩ C) = P(A) × P(B | A) × P(C | A ∩ B).

The fact that the overlap can be written two ways is what leads to Bayes' theorem in the next file.

The law of total probability

Suppose every customer is either a coffee buyer or not. Then the overall chance of a pastry is a weighted average of the chance within each group, weighted by group size:

P(A) = P(A | B) P(B) + P(A | Bc) P(Bc)

Bc means "not B". In the square above, this just says the pastry area is the pastry block in the left column plus the pastry block in the right column.

One consequence: the overall rate always sits between the two conditional rates. Slide the coffee share and watch the overall pastry rate move toward whichever group is bigger.

Independence

Events A and B are independent when learning about one tells you nothing about the other. Any one of these three statements says so, and each implies the others (when the probabilities involved are positive):

P(A | B) = P(A)
P(B | A) = P(B)
P(A ∩ B) = P(A) × P(B)
Independent is not the same as mutually exclusive. Mutually exclusive events cannot happen together, so P(A ∩ B) = 0. If you learn that B happened, you know A did not. That is the strongest possible dependence, not independence.

Conditional probabilities are still probabilities

Once you fix B, the numbers P( · | B) follow all the usual rules. They are never negative, the whole space has probability 1, and complements work as normal:

P(Ac | B) = 1 − P(A | B)

Be careful about which side of the bar the complement is on. P(A | Bc) is not 1 − P(A | B). It describes a different group of customers entirely, which here is 10%, not 60%.

Common mistakes

  • Swapping the two sides. P(A | B) and P(B | A) share a numerator but divide by different groups. "If the defendant is innocent, this evidence is rare" is not the same claim as "given this evidence, the defendant is probably guilty". Courts have a name for confusing them: the prosecutor's fallacy.
  • Hearing "given" as "because". Coffee buyers being more likely to buy pastries does not mean the coffee caused the pastry. Both could come from a morning rush. Conditional probability describes association, not cause.
  • Dividing by the wrong group. Always ask which customers you are still considering once you know what you know. That group is the denominator.

What to take away

Conditioning on an event throws away every outcome where it did not happen. What is left becomes the new whole, so you divide by its size: P(A | B) = P(A and B) ÷ P(B).

P(pastry | coffee) and P(coffee | pastry) are different questions with different answers. They share the same numerator, the customers who bought both, but divide by different groups.

If learning B never changes the chance of A, the two events are independent. Use the button above to see what that looks like.
