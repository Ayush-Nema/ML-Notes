The owner of the Corner Café has years of records: with the old machine, customers waited 5.0 minutes on average, and individual waits typically varied by about 1.5 minutes around that. After installing a new machine, she timed 36 customers. Their average was 4.5 minutes.

Half a minute faster. Is the new machine genuinely better, or did she just happen to time a quiet afternoon? Hypothesis testing is a fair, step-by-step way to decide.

The big idea

A hypothesis test works like a trial

In a courtroom the defendant is presumed innocent. The jury does not need to prove innocence. It only asks whether the evidence is so strong that "innocent" becomes hard to believe. If it is, they convict. If it is not, they say "not guilty", which does not mean the defendant is proven innocent. It means the evidence fell short.

Hypothesis testing uses the same logic. We start by assuming the boring explanation (nothing changed), then check whether our data would be very surprising under that assumption.

IntuitionWhy start by assuming nothing changed? Because "nothing changed" is a precise claim. It lets us calculate exactly what kinds of results luck alone would produce. "Something changed" is vague (by how much?), so we cannot calculate with it directly.
Step 1 of 5

State two competing hypotheses

First, one piece of vocabulary. The symbol μ (say "mew") stands for the true long-run average wait with the new machine, the number we would get if we could time every customer, forever. We can never see μ directly. All we get is a small sample.

H₀ null hypothesis
μ = 5.0 minutes

The new machine made no difference. The true average wait is still 5.0. Any gap in our sample is just luck.

H₁ alternative hypothesis
μ ≠ 5.0 minutes

The new machine changed the average wait. This is the claim the owner hopes the data can support.

IntuitionH₀ always contains an equals sign and says "nothing is going on". It is the claim we try to knock down. H₁ is what we conclude only if the evidence forces us to.

Two habits worth building from day one. Write both hypotheses before looking at the data, so you cannot tailor them to what you see. And remember that they are claims about the true average μ, never about the 36 customers in the sample. We already know the sample average is 4.5. The real question is what that says about μ.

H₁ here says "different", which allows the wait to be longer or shorter. If the owner only cared about "faster", she could instead write H₁: μ < 5.0. You can switch between the two in Step 5.

Simplification for this page: we treat the spread of 1.5 minutes as known from the café's past records, and assume waits are roughly bell-shaped. Real studies often have to estimate the spread from the sample and use a slightly different table (the t-test). The logic stays the same.

Step 2 of 5

Collect a sample, and meet luck

The owner's sample average was 4.5. If H₀ is true and the real average is still 5.0, how likely is a sample average that far off? To answer that, we need a feel for how much a sample average wobbles from one sample to the next purely by chance.

Three things to notice. The averages pile up around 5.0, the true value. They spread out symmetrically in a bell shape. And switching from 9 to 36 to 100 customers per sample makes the pile narrower: bigger samples wobble less.

Intuition: the standard errorOne customer might wait 2 minutes or 9. But an average over many customers smooths out the extremes, because the unusually long waits get cancelled by unusually short ones. The typical size of the wobble in a sample average is called the standard error (SE), and it equals σ / √n, the spread of single waits divided by the square root of the sample size. With σ = 1.5 and n = 36, SE = 1.5 / 6 = 0.25 minutes. That is the "yardstick" we use next.

Look at the share of simulated groups that landed as far from 5.0 as the owner's 4.5. Even in a world where nothing changed, that happens some of the time. That share is the heart of the next two steps.

Step 3 of 5

Turn the difference into a test statistic

The owner's average was 0.5 minutes below 5.0. Is 0.5 minutes a lot? It depends on the yardstick. A 10-minute delay is nothing for a long-haul flight and huge for a bus that comes every 5 minutes. Here the right yardstick is the standard error, the normal size of the wobble.

So we measure the gap in units of "wobbles". This number is the test statistic, usually called z:

Reading zA z near 0 means the sample is right where H₀ says it should be. As a rough guide, a z beyond about ±2 is unusual (it happens by luck about 1 time in 20), and beyond ±3 is very unusual. The sign only tells you the direction: negative means below 5.0, which here means faster.

Try a few things. Keep the sample average at 4.5 and drag n down to 9. The same half-minute gap now equals only 1 wobble, because small samples wobble a lot. Drag n up to 150 and the same gap becomes a much stronger signal. The amount of evidence depends on both the size of the gap and the size of the sample.

Step 4 of 5

Find the p-value

Now we ask the central question. If H₀ were true (nothing changed), how often would luck alone produce a result at least as extreme as ours? That probability is the p-value.

The bell curve below shows how z behaves when H₀ is true. Most of the time z lands near 0. Large positive or negative values are rare, and the rarest live in the thin "tails" on either side. The shaded area is the p-value: the share of the curve at least as far out as our z.

Why both tails?Our H₁ says the wait changed. A sample average of 5.5 would have been just as convincing a sign of change as 4.5, only in the opposite direction. So "at least as extreme" counts both sides, and the p-value adds up both shaded tails.
Connect it to Step 2Back in the simulation, you saw what share of the pretend groups landed as far from 5.0 as the owner's. That share is the p-value, estimated by brute force. The math in this step gives the exact answer without needing thousands of pretend groups.
What the p-value is notA p-value of 0.046 does not mean "there's a 4.6% chance the machine does nothing". It is calculated assuming the machine does nothing, so it cannot also be the probability of that assumption. It only measures how well the data fits the "nothing changed" story: small p means the data fits it poorly.
Step 5 of 5

Make the decision

A small p-value means our data would be surprising if nothing had changed. But how small is small enough? Before collecting data, we choose a cutoff called the significance level, α (alpha). It is the line in the sand for "too surprising to blame on luck".

The rule is simple:

  • If p ≤ α, reject H₀. The data is too surprising to be blamed on luck.
  • If p > α, fail to reject H₀. The data is plausible under "nothing changed", so we are not convinced.

The most common choice is α = 0.05. It means we accept being fooled by luck about 1 time in 20 when nothing is really going on. A stricter α (0.01) demands stronger evidence. A looser α (0.10) is easier to satisfy.

One-sided or two-sided?If you switch H₁ to "faster" (μ < 5.0), all of α goes into the left tail only, so the cutoff is closer to 0 and a "faster" result is easier to confirm. The price is that you can no longer detect a machine that made things slower. Choose the direction honestly, before seeing the data, based on the question you actually care about.
Notice the owner's resultWith the original numbers the p-value sits just under 0.05, so the verdict is a narrow "reject". Switch α to 0.01 and the same data no longer convinces us. That is why α must be fixed in advance: otherwise one could pick whichever cutoff gives the answer one wants.

How to word the conclusion. If we reject H₀: "The data gives statistically significant evidence that the average wait has changed." If we fail to reject: "We do not have enough evidence to say the average wait has changed." We avoid saying "we proved the machine works" or "we proved it makes no difference". A test weighs evidence. It does not deliver proof.

The last preset is worth a look. A small 0.2-minute gap (12 seconds) becomes "statistically significant" once 400 customers are timed, even though few owners would care about 12 seconds. Statistical significance and practical importance are different questions.

Beyond the steps

Tests can be wrong, in two different ways

A test works from a sample, so sometimes it points the wrong way. There are exactly two kinds of mistake, depending on what is really true:

Our decisionReality: machine changed nothingReality: machine really is faster
Reject H₀
"It changed"		Type I error (false alarm). We celebrate a machine that does nothing. Chance = α.Correct. We detected a real improvement. Chance = power.
Fail to reject H₀
"Not convinced"		Correct. We held back when there was nothing to find.Type II error (miss). We overlook a real improvement. Chance = β.

Choosing α is choosing how many false alarms you will tolerate. A smaller α means fewer false alarms but more misses. Think of a smoke detector: make it less sensitive and it stops going off when you make toast, but it may also be slower to catch a real fire.

Beyond the steps

Four common misunderstandings

Tempting beliefWhat is actually true
"p = 0.03 means a 3% chance that H₀ is true."The p-value assumes H₀ is true and measures how surprising the data would be. It says nothing directly about the chance that H₀ is true.
"We failed to reject H₀, so the machine makes no difference."Failing to reject means the evidence was not strong enough. A small sample can easily miss a real effect. "Absence of evidence" is not "evidence of absence".
"Significant means important."With enough data, even a trivial difference becomes significant. Always ask how big the difference is and whether it matters in practice (a 12-second faster wait may not justify a new machine).
"I'll keep testing until p drops below 0.05."Run enough tests and luck will eventually hand you a small p. Decide the hypotheses, α and sample size before looking at the data.
