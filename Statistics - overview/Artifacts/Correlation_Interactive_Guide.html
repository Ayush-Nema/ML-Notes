What correlation is

A single number between −1 and +1 that says how two variables move together.

The idea

Picture a scatter plot with one dot per person: hours studied on the x-axis, quiz score on the y-axis. The correlation coefficient packs two things into one number.

  • The sign is the direction. Positive means they rise together. Negative means one rises as the other falls.
  • The size (distance from 0) is the strength: how tightly the dots hug the pattern.

It does not tell you how steep the pattern is. That is the slope, a different idea.

Pearson correlation

How well do the points follow a straight line? This is what people mean when they just say "correlation".

The idea

Draw a vertical line at the average of x and a horizontal line at the average of y. That splits the plot into four quadrants, and every dot casts a vote.

  • Top-right or bottom-left: above average in both, or below in both. The dot votes positive.
  • Top-left or bottom-right: above in one, below in the other. The dot votes negative.

Pearson is essentially the average vote, with bigger votes from dots far from the center.

Try it: quadrant votes

Drag dots between quadrants. When the votes cancel out, r heads toward 0.

The math

  • Top: for each dot, multiply how far x is from its mean by how far y is from its mean, then add them all up. That is the quadrant vote.
  • Bottom: divide by the overall spread of x and of y, so the answer ignores units and always lands between −1 and 1.

Two equivalent forms worth seeing once. The first uses covariance (the raw vote, which depends on units) and the standard deviations. The second averages the product of z-scores (distance from the mean, in standard deviations).

Properties: r is always between −1 and 1. Changing units does not change it. Swapping x and y does not change it. Flipping the sign of one variable flips the sign of r.

Units do not matter

Covariance changes when you change units. Pearson r does not. Switch units and compare.

Spearman correlation

Replace the raw values with their ranks, then compute Pearson on the ranks.

The idea

Imagine a race where you only record finish positions (1st, 2nd, 3rd) instead of finish times. Ranks forget "by how much" and keep only "who is ahead of whom".

  • Two rankings in nearly the same order: Spearman near +1.
  • One ranking the exact reverse of the other: −1.
  • Orderings that look unrelated: near 0.

Because it only uses order, a curve that always rises scores exactly 1, and a huge outlier is just "the biggest value".

Try it: from raw values to ranks

Months since launch against daily signups. Drag a dot, then convert to ranks and watch the table.

The math

Ties: when values tie, each gets the average of the ranks it would have taken. Type some numbers to see.

Shortcut when there are no ties: let d be the difference between the two ranks of a point.

If the ranks match exactly, every d is 0 and ρ = 1. The bigger the rank disagreements, the smaller ρ gets.

Try it: why outliers hurt Pearson more

Make the biggest signup number larger. The order of the dots never changes, so Spearman stays put.

Cautions and how it is used in ML

A correlation is evidence of a pattern, not an explanation of it.

Correlation is not causation

Ice cream sales and sunburns are correlated. Hot sunny weather drives both. A hidden driver like that is a confounder. Whenever X and Y are correlated there are three explanations: X causes Y, Y causes X, or a third variable causes both (or it is coincidence).

Try it: hold temperature roughly fixed and see what happens to the link.

Small samples are noisy

Unrelated variables still show some correlation by luck. Pick a sample size and run 2,000 pairs of completely unrelated random numbers.

Cheat sheet

Putting it together

  1. Make a scatter plot first, always.
  2. Correlation compresses "do they move together?" into one number from −1 to +1: sign is direction, size is strength.
  3. Pearson measures linear association using raw values.
  4. Spearman measures monotonic association using only ranks, so it handles curves, skew and outliers better.
  5. Compute both when unsure. A big gap is a hint to look at the plot.
  6. A coefficient from few points is noisy. Check the sample size and the p-value.
  7. Correlation is not causation, and r = 0 does not mean "unrelated".
