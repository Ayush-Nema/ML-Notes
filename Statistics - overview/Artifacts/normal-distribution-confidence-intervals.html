The normal distribution, and how it lets us estimate with confidence

This guide has two parts. Part 1 introduces the bell curve through a coffee shop: what it looks like, what its two numbers mean, and how to read probabilities from it. Part 2 shows why the bell curve matters so much: when you only see a sample, the normal distribution lets you say how far your estimate might be from the truth. That is the idea behind confidence intervals.

Part 1: The normal distribution

A coffee shop, a few thousand customers and one very useful shape.

What is a normal distribution?

You own a coffee shop called Bean & Brew. Hundreds of customers visit every day. Some spend ₹100, some ₹200, a few ₹400, but most spend somewhere around ₹250. You start wondering:

  • Why do most customers spend around ₹250?
  • Why do very few spend extremely little or extremely much?
  • Can we predict what percentage of customers spend between ₹200 and ₹300?

A normal distribution describes exactly this situation: most observations sit close to the average, and observations further away become steadily rarer. The pattern is symmetric, and the picture is a bell. Below, every customer is simulated from a normal distribution with an average of ₹250. Add more customers and use narrower bars, and the bars trace out the bell.

Customers at Bean & Brew

A fair warning. Not every real-world measurement is normal. Real customer spending is often skewed, because a few customers spend unusually large amounts. We use an idealized example to learn the concept. Always check that data actually looks bell-shaped before relying on a normal model.

Two numbers describe it: mean and standard deviation

Mean (μ): where is the center?

The average. At Bean & Brew, μ = ₹250. Move the mean and the whole bell slides left or right without changing shape.

Standard deviation (σ): how spread out?

How far spending typically strays from the average. Here σ = ₹50. A small σ gives a tall, narrow bell, and a large σ gives a low, wide one.

We write X ~ N(μ, σ²), read "X is normally distributed with mean μ and variance σ²". Try the sliders.

Remember: the mean decides the center, and the standard deviation decides the spread. Knowing the average alone is not enough. We also need to know how observations spread around it.

The 68–95–99.7 rule

The most useful shortcut for reading a bell curve.

Bean & Brew: μ = ₹250, σ = ₹50

Pick how many standard deviations either side of the average to look at.

inside the bandoutside
  • 68% of customers spend between ₹200 and ₹300 (within 1 SD).
  • 95% spend between ₹150 and ₹350 (within 2 SDs).
  • 99.7% spend between ₹100 and ₹400 (within 3 SDs).

Using it. What percentage of customers spend more than ₹300? ₹300 is one SD above the average. About 68% fall within one SD, so about 32% fall outside. The curve is symmetric, so half of that 32% is on the high side:

P(X > 300) ≈ (100% − 68%) ÷ 2 ≈ 16%

Roughly 16 of every 100 customers spend more than ₹300. The calculator in the next section gives the exact figure.

Why the chance of one exact amount is zero

Binomial and Poisson distributions count things (0, 1, 2, 3 customers), so each count has its own probability. A normal distribution is continuous: spending could be ₹250.00, ₹250.001, and so on forever, so any one exact value has zero chance. We always ask about ranges. Shrink the range around ₹250 and watch the probability vanish.

The z-score: how unusual is this value?

A customer spends ₹325. Is that unusually high? A z-score says how many standard deviations a value is above or below the mean:

z = X − μσ = 325 − 25050 = 1.5

So ₹325 is 1.5 standard deviations above average. Z = 0 means exactly average, z = +1 is one SD above, z = −1 is one SD below, z = +2 is two SDs above. Because z-scores are in "SD units", they work for any normal distribution: heights, delivery times or exam scores.

Where does a customer stand?

Typical rule of thumb: |z| under 1 is ordinary, between 1 and 2 is notable, over 2 is unusual, over 3 is very rare.

Where normal fits among the three distributions

DistributionWhat it describesCoffee shop question

Approximately normal models can help with coffee preparation times, product quality (how much a machine dispenses), manufacturing measurements, and bell-shaped exam scores.

Part 2: From a sample to a confidence interval

Why the normal distribution matters even when your data is not normal, and how to turn one sample into an honest range of plausible values.

The business problem: population vs sample

A food-delivery company wants the average delivery time of all orders in Bengaluru this month. There were 1,000,000 orders. Checking all of them is often impractical: collecting data costs money, measurements may need surveys, some measurements are destructive, and the whole population may simply not be available.

So the company examines n = 100 orders and finds an average of 32 minutes. Does that mean the true average of all one million orders is exactly 32 minutes? No. We are trying to use a sample to learn about a population while being honest about our uncertainty.

Population

Every delivery completed this month. Its mean μ and standard deviation σ are called parameters. We usually want them, but they are unknown.

Sample

100 randomly selected deliveries. Its mean x̄ and standard deviation s are called statistics. We can compute them.

Use statistics (x̄, s) to estimate parameters (μ, σ)

Why the sampling method matters

Suppose you want average customer satisfaction, but you survey only customers who left 5-star reviews. The sample will look wonderful, and it will be biased. Try it.

Four popular methods

Simple randomEvery member has an equal chance. Example: pick 500 random IDs from 10,000 customers. Usually the cleanest starting point.
StratifiedSplit into important subgroups (region, age group, Android vs iOS) and sample from each. Use it when subgroups must be represented.
SystematicTake every k-th item, such as every 50th invoice. Easy, but biased if the data has a repeating pattern.
ClusterRandomly choose whole groups, such as 10 of 100 cities, and survey inside them. Cheaper when the population is naturally grouped.

Sampling distribution: the sample mean has its own distribution

Suppose the true average delivery time is μ = 30 minutes. Take 100 deliveries and get x̄₁ = 29.6. Take another 100: x̄₂ = 30.8. Another: 29.9. Another: 30.4. Why aren't all the sample means exactly 30? Because sampling is random.

Now imagine repeating this 10,000 times: each time take n deliveries and compute the average. Plot all those averages. That picture is the sampling distribution of the sample mean.

Be careful. It is not the distribution of individual delivery times. It is the distribution of x̄ across many hypothetical samples. Two different histograms are in play below, so keep them apart.

Two things to notice. The sample means are centered on the true average (30), and they are much more tightly clustered than individual deliveries. Bigger samples squeeze the cluster tighter. The spread of the sample means has a name: the standard error, introduced shortly.

The Central Limit Theorem

Here is why the normal distribution matters in Part 2. Many real-world measurements are roughly normal, but more importantly, sample means often become approximately normal, even when the original data is not.

If we repeatedly take sufficiently large independent random samples, the distribution of the sample means tends to look approximately normal, even when the population is not.

Customer purchases are typically heavily right-skewed: most people spend little, a few spend a lot. Definitely not normal. Yet the averages of repeated samples form a bell. Pick a population below and increase n.

What CLT does not say

It does not say your raw data becomes normal. Individual purchases stay skewed forever. It says the distribution of sample means tends toward normal.

Why we care

We know a great deal about the normal distribution, such as the 68–95–99.7 rule. That lets us reason about how close a sample mean is likely to be to μ, which leads straight to standard error and confidence intervals.

Standard error: how much does the sample mean wobble?

If repeated sample means come out as 29.9, 30.1, 30.0, 29.8, 30.2, they are tightly clustered, and our estimate is stable. If they come out as 26, 32, 28, 35, 29, our estimate depends heavily on which sample we happened to draw. We need one number for "how much would the sample mean vary from sample to sample?" That number is the standard error.

SE = σ√n   (usually estimated by s√n, since σ is unknown)
Standard deviation (SD)Standard error (SE)
Variation among individual observationsVariation among sample means
Describes the dataDescribes the uncertainty in an estimate
Does not shrink as the sample growsShrinks as the sample grows

SD asks, "how different are individuals?" SE asks, "how uncertain is my estimated mean?"

Why sample size matters

Business takeaway: to cut the standard error in half you need about 4 times the sample size. Precision gets expensive: going from 25 to 100 observations halves the uncertainty, but halving it again needs 400.

Confidence intervals: the core idea

Instead of reporting only "average delivery time = 32 minutes", we can report: "Our estimate is 32 minutes, and a plausible range for the population mean is roughly 30 to 34 minutes." That range is a confidence interval. It always has the same structure:

Estimate ± Margin of error
x̄ ± (critical value) × SE

This single recipe unifies everything that follows. The critical value comes from the normal (or t) distribution and says how many standard errors to extend on each side.

What does "95% confident" actually mean?

A common misreading is "there is a 95% probability that μ lies inside this interval." In the standard interpretation μ is a fixed number, and it is the procedure that has the 95%:

If we repeated the whole sampling procedure many times and built a 95% interval each time, about 95% of those intervals would contain the true mean.

The z method (σ known)

In the classic textbook setup the population standard deviation σ is known (or the sample is large enough that a normal approximation is justified). For a 95% interval the critical value is z* = 1.96:

CI = x̄ ± z* × σ√n

Where does 1.96 come from? For a standard normal distribution, about 95% of the probability lies between −1.96 and +1.96 standard deviations. So a 95% interval extends 1.96 standard errors each side of the estimate. Move the confidence level below to see the shaded central area and its critical value.

The t-distribution: when σ is unknown

In real life we almost never know σ. We only have the sample, so we estimate it with s. That adds extra uncertainty, and we account for it by using the t-distribution instead of the normal.

A t-distribution looks like a normal distribution but with heavier tails: it gives more probability to extreme values. Its exact shape depends on the degrees of freedom, which for a one-sample mean is df = n − 1. Small samples carry little information about variability (small df, heavy tails). Large samples carry plenty, and t becomes almost exactly normal.

Critical values: t* versus z*

The critical value you multiply by SE is larger for t, especially with few degrees of freedom. The bottom row (df = ∞) is the normal distribution's z*.

The t interval

The structure never changed. Only the critical value and the standard deviation used differ:

Z (σ known)
x̄ ± z* · σ/√n
T (σ unknown, use s)
x̄ ± t* · s/√n

It is always estimate ± critical value × standard error. The default example below samples 25 customers with an average spend of $80 and a sample standard deviation of $20.

Does it really matter? Test the claim

Using z* with an estimated s ignores the extra uncertainty. With small samples the intervals come out too narrow and fail to capture μ as often as promised. Compare methods below: with n = 5 and "z with s plugged in" selected, a "95%" interval captures the true mean noticeably less than 95% of the time. The t interval fixes it.

Margin of error: what controls it?

ME = (critical value) × SE   and   CI = estimate ± ME
Z or T?
  • Know σ → use Z.
  • Don't know σ (the usual case) → use T.
  • Large sample, σ unknown: T still works and is almost identical to Z.
Three levers on the margin of error
  • Larger n → smaller SE → smaller ME.
  • More variable data (larger σ or s) → larger ME.
  • Higher confidence level → larger ME.

An ML connection: is Model B really better?

Model A scores 84% accuracy and Model B scores 85%. Is B definitely better? Both numbers come from a finite test set. With a different test set the accuracies would change slightly, so model performance is itself an estimate. It deserves an interval: "85% ± 2%" rather than just "85%".

For an accuracy (a proportion), the standard error has the form √(p(1 − p) ÷ n), and the interval is the same recipe: estimate ± 1.96 × SE.

Compare two models

The same logic applies to precision and recall, A/B tests, business metrics and model comparisons. (A formal comparison of two models would test the difference directly, but intervals already show that a one-point gap on a small test set can be well within the noise.)

Key takeaways

  • A normal distribution is described by its mean (center) and standard deviation (spread). About 68%, 95% and 99.7% of values lie within 1, 2 and 3 SDs.
  • A z-score says how many SDs a value is from the mean.
  • A sample mean is an estimate. It wobbles from sample to sample, and the standard error σ/√n measures how much.
  • The central limit theorem says sample means are approximately normal even when the data is not, which is why normal-based intervals work so broadly.
  • Every confidence interval is estimate ± critical value × SE. Use z* when σ is known, t* (with df = n − 1) when it is not.
  • "95% confident" is a statement about the procedure: 95% of intervals built this way capture the true value.
