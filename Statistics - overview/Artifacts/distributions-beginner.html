Three distributions, explained with examples

Some results are more likely than others, and a distribution is simply the picture of how likely each result is. This guide covers three of the most useful ones: binomial (counting yes/no results), Poisson (counting events over time) and normal (the bell curve for measurements). Every idea starts with an everyday example you can click, and formulas are tucked into optional boxes.

1. BinomialHow many of 10 coin flips land heads? How many of 25 parts are defective? 2. PoissonHow many calls does a help desk get in an hour? How many typos are on a page? 3. NormalHow tall are adults? How are exam scores or machine-made parts spread out?

Warm-up: what is a distribution?

Start with something you already know: rolling two dice.

Roll two dice and add them up. You can get anything from 2 to 12, but the totals are not equally likely. A total of 7 can be made six ways (1+6, 2+5, 3+4, 4+3, 5+2, 6+1), while a 2 can only be made one way (1+1). The list of all possible totals with how likely each one is is the distribution.

Four words you will see

ProbabilityHow likely something is, from 0% (never) to 100% (always). A 1-in-4 chance is 25%.
Average (mean)The balance point of the distribution. For two dice the average total is 7.
Spread (standard deviation)How far results typically stray from the average. Wide spread means less predictable.
IndependentOne result does not affect another. Your first coin flip tells you nothing about the second.

Counting vs. measuring. Dice totals and coin flips are counts (whole numbers), so each result has its own probability. A height or a weight is a measurement: it can be any value on a smooth scale, so we talk about the chance of falling in a range instead. Binomial and Poisson are for counts. Normal is for measurements.

1. Binomial distribution

Use it when you repeat the same yes/no event a fixed number of times and count the yeses.

The idea in one example

You flip a coin 10 times and count the heads. Each flip is a trial with two outcomes: heads (a "success") or tails. The count of heads can be anything from 0 to 10, and the binomial distribution tells you how likely each count is. The same recipe works for free throws, emails opened, defective parts and guessed quiz answers.

It fits when all four of these are true: each trial is yes/no; the number of trials is fixed in advance; trials do not affect each other; and the chance of success is the same each time.

Binomial fits
  • Heads in 10 coin flips
  • Baskets made out of 10 free throws
  • People who open an email, out of 20 sent
  • Defective parts in a box of 25
  • Correct answers when guessing on a quiz
Binomial does not fit
  • Customers per day: nobody fixed the number of trials (that is Poisson)
  • Cards drawn from a small deck without putting them back: the chances change after each draw
  • Friends who all decide together to open an email: not independent
  • A person's height: a measurement, not a count of yes/no results

Why some counts are more likely than others

Flip a coin a few times and ask for exactly k heads. Every different sequence that gives k heads counts. There are more sequences with a mix of heads and tails than with all heads, and that is why middle results are more likely. Change the sliders to list every sequence.

What shape does it take?

  • Coin flips (50%): a symmetric hill centered on 5.
  • Defects (2%): the pile sits at 0 and trails off to the right, because successes are rare.
  • Free throws (70%): the pile leans toward the high end, because success is common.
  • More trials: the hill gets smoother and more bell-shaped. Load the coin example and raise the number of flips to 100 to see it.

Average and spread. The average number of successes is simply n × p. Flip 10 coins at 50%: you expect 5 heads. Send 20 emails at a 30% open rate: you expect 6 opens. The actual count will wander around that average by a few, which the "typical spread" card shows.

Peek at the math (optional)

The chance of exactly k successes in n trials is:

P(k) = (number of ways to choose which trials succeed) × pk × (1 − p)n − k

Worked example. Exactly 2 heads in 4 fair flips. There are 6 sequences with 2 heads (see the demo above). Each has probability 0.5 × 0.5 × 0.5 × 0.5 = 0.0625. So P(2 heads) = 6 × 0.0625 = 0.375, or 37.5%.

The typical spread is √(n × p × (1 − p)). For 10 fair coin flips that is √(10 × 0.5 × 0.5) ≈ 1.6, so you usually land within 1 or 2 of 5.

Common mistakes. Using it when trials affect each other (people copying each other's choices), when the chance changes from trial to trial (a mix of keen and casual customers), or when you did not fix the number of trials in advance.

2. Poisson distribution

Use it when you count how many times something happens over a stretch of time or space, and there is no fixed number of trials.

The idea in one example

A help desk gets about 4 calls an hour on average. In any particular hour it might get 2, or 7, or none. There is no "number of trials" this time: a call could come at any moment, and nothing stops it from going higher. The Poisson distribution gives the chance of each count, and it needs just one number: the average count per interval (called λ, "lambda").

It fits when events happen one at a time, independently of each other, at a steady average rate.

Poisson fits
  • Calls arriving at a help desk per hour
  • Typos on a page
  • Shooting stars seen per hour on a clear night
  • Website errors per day
  • Potholes per kilometer of road
Poisson does not fit well
  • Traffic that is far heavier at rush hour: the rate is not steady
  • Aftershocks: one event makes more events likely
  • Calls that arrive in bunches (a whole office calling at once)
  • Counts with a hard upper limit, like seats on a bus

Why does Poisson exist?

Cut the hour into equal slices. In each slice a call either arrives or it does not, which is a yes/no trial, so the count of calls is a binomial. Use smaller and smaller slices (here the average is always 4 calls per hour) and watch it turn into a Poisson.

Things worth knowing

  • The average and the spread are linked. If the average is 4, the typical spread is about 2 (its square root). So a busier desk is more variable in absolute terms, but less variable compared with its size.
  • Quiet spells happen. Even with an average of 4 per hour, an hour with zero calls happens now and then. Use "Exactly 0" to see how often.
  • At least one. The chance of at least one event is 1 − (chance of none). It is often the number you really want.
  • Streams combine. Two independent streams with averages 3 and 5 per hour combine into one stream averaging 8 per hour, still Poisson.
Peek at the math (optional)

The chance of exactly k events when the average is λ:

P(k) = e−λ × λk ÷ k!

Worked example. Average 4 calls an hour, chance of no calls: e−4 × 40 ÷ 0! = e−4 ≈ 0.018, about 1.8%. The number e ≈ 2.718 is a constant that shows up whenever things grow or decay continuously.

Where it comes from: take a binomial with a huge number of slices n and a tiny chance p per slice, keeping n × p = λ. As n grows, the binomial formula simplifies to exactly the one above.

Common mistakes. Assuming a constant rate when it really changes during the day, and ignoring bunching. A quick check: for real Poisson counts, the variance (spread squared) is about equal to the average. If it is much larger, events are clumping.

3. Normal distribution

The bell curve. Use it for measurements that cluster around an average, and for totals of many small effects.

The idea in one example

Think of adult heights. Most people are close to the average, a few are much shorter or taller, and the pattern is symmetric: just as many far below as far above. Plotted, that is a smooth bell. Two numbers describe it completely: the average (where the middle is) and the spread (how wide the bell is).

Because height can be any value, the chance of being exactly 170.000 cm is essentially zero. Instead we ask about ranges, like "between 160 and 180 cm", and the answer is the area under the curve over that range.

Roughly normal
  • Adult heights
  • Measurement errors
  • Parts made by a machine (bolt diameters, fill volumes)
  • The total of many dice rolls
Usually not normal
  • Incomes: a few huge values stretch the right side
  • Waiting times: bunched near zero with a long tail
  • Star ratings: stuck between 1 and 5
  • Counts of rare events (use Poisson or binomial)

The 68–95–99.7 rule of thumb

For any bell curve, about 68% of values land within 1 spread of the average, about 95% within 2 spreads, and about 99.7% within 3 spreads. With heights (average 170, spread 10): about 68% of adults are between 160 and 180 cm, about 95% between 150 and 190, and nearly everyone between 140 and 200. Tick the box in the explorer to see the bands for each example.

How many spreads away is a value? That count is called a z-score. A height of 190 is 2 spreads above average, so z = +2. It tells you how unusual a value is in a way that works for any example: heights, test scores or bolts. The explorer's "Where does one value stand?" slider turns it into "higher than X% of values".

Why does the bell curve show up everywhere?

When you add up many small random pieces, the total tends to form a bell, almost no matter what a single piece looks like. This is called the central limit theorem. Pick a single draw, then add more and more of them.

Things worth knowing

  • Symmetric and centered. The average, the middle value and the most common value are all the same point.
  • Spread sets the width. A machine with a small spread makes parts that are all nearly identical. Try shrinking the spread on the bolts example and watch the share inside the limits climb.
  • The center matters too. Even a low-spread machine fails if its average drifts away from the target. Slide the average on the bolts example.
  • Spreads combine in a surprising way. Stack three bolts, each with spread 0.05 mm. The stack's spread is about 0.087 mm, not 0.15: random errors partly cancel.
Peek at the math (optional)

A z-score is just how many spreads a value is from the average:

z = (value − average) ÷ spread

Worked example. Heights average 170 cm with spread 10 cm. A person 185 cm tall has z = (185 − 170) ÷ 10 = 1.5, so they are 1.5 spreads above average. A table (or any spreadsheet function) says about 93% of people are shorter.

The curve itself is given by f(x) = 1 ÷ (spread × √(2π)) × e−(x − average)² ÷ (2 × spread²). You never need to compute it by hand: the explorer does it for you.

Common mistakes. Assuming everything is normal (incomes and waiting times are not). Forgetting that the bell curve's tails are thin, so extreme values are very rare if the data really is normal, but much more common if it is not. And remembering that a normal model happily allows impossible values, like a negative height, with a tiny probability.

Putting it together

The three are relatives, not strangers.

  • Binomial → Poisson. Chop an hour into many tiny slices with a small chance of an event in each. That is a binomial, and it behaves like a Poisson.
  • Binomial → normal. With lots of trials and a success rate that is not extreme, the binomial's bars form a bell. A count of heads in 100 flips looks like a bell curve.
  • Poisson → normal. When the average count is large (say 20+), the Poisson bars also form a bell.

This is useful: when a binomial is awkward to work out, a Poisson or normal curve can stand in for it. The lab below shows when that works.

Notes and sources

The definitions and relationships here are standard results. Good general references:

  • J. K. Blitzstein and J. Hwang, Introduction to Probability (CRC Press), a friendly, example-driven textbook.
  • NIST/SEMATECH e-Handbook of Statistical Methods, section on probability distributions: itl.nist.gov/div898/handbook.
  • G. Casella and R. L. Berger, Statistical Inference (Cengage), for the more mathematical treatment.

What is a guideline. The "stand-in" checks in the lab (normal works when you expect at least about 10 successes and 10 failures; Poisson works when there are many trials and successes are rare) are common rules of thumb. Textbooks vary on the exact cutoffs, so treat them as rough guides.

About the examples. All numbers (open rates, defect rates, call rates, heights, scores, bolt sizes) are made up for illustration and are not measured data. Simulations use your browser's pseudo-random generator, so results change each time. I have not verified specific page numbers in the references; check the editions you have.
