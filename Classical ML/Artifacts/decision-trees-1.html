01 / The big idea

A flowchart that learns its questions from data.

A decision tree predicts by following a sequence of yes/no questions. Each question sorts examples into smaller groups; a final group gives the prediction.

1

Start with examples

Each row has features (the clues) and a label (the answer to learn).

2

Ask a useful question

Pick a feature and split the rows so the answers on each side are more alike.

3

Follow the path

For a new row, answer the questions until you reach a leaf with a prediction.

Vocabulary: A root is the first question, a branch is an answer to a question, and a leaf is a final prediction. Here we teach classification (choosing a category); trees can also predict numbers, called regression.

How is a split scored?

Gini impurity = 1 − (fraction Yes)² − (fraction No)²0 means all labels match. For two balanced classes, the score is 0.5. A split's score is the average child impurity weighted by child size. Lower is better.

For example, a leaf with 3 Yes and 1 No has impurity 1 − (3/4)² − (1/4)² = 0.375. Ties can be broken in different ways by different implementations.

When does it stop? A node stops when its labels agree, no available split improves it, or a limit such as maximum depth or minimum leaf size is reached. The leaf predicts its most common class. A small tree is easier to explain; a large tree can memorize quirks in training data.

05 / Check what it learned

Judge it on flowers it has not seen.

Training accuracy tells us how well a tree fits known examples. Test accuracy estimates how well it handles new examples. The same held-out flowers are used at every depth here.

Training vs test accuracy

Accuracy = correct predictions ÷ all predictions. A higher training score alone does not establish better generalization.

Confusion matrix · test flowers

Rows are actual species; columns are predicted species. Green diagonal cells are correct. Other cells show the kinds of mistakes.

Beyond accuracy

Precision: among flowers predicted as a species, how many really are that species?

Recall: among flowers that really are a species, how many did we find?

F1: a score combining precision and recall. “Macro” gives each species equal weight.

Why keep the test set separate?

If you repeatedly tune a model based on its test score, the test set starts influencing your choices. In a real project, use a validation set or cross-validation to choose depth, then use a separate test set for a final check.

Our depth slider invites exploration, so treat the displayed test score as a teaching illustration, not a final unbiased performance estimate after tuning.

06 / What to remember

Simple rules. Real tradeoffs.

Decision trees are intuitive, but controlling their size and evaluating them carefully matters.

Where they shine

Merits

✓ Easy to explain by tracing a path from root to leaf.

✓ Accept numeric and categorical clues (with suitable encoding for categories, depending on the implementation).

✓ Learn nonlinear boundaries and interactions without manually writing those rules.

✓ Usually need little feature scaling.

Where to be careful

Limitations

! Deep trees can overfit: they learn accidents of the training set.

! A small data change can produce a different tree.

! Splits favor simple axis-aligned questions, which may need many branches for some patterns.

! Class imbalance or unequal error costs may make accuracy misleading.

Pruning and regularization: Limit maximum depth or minimum leaf size while learning (pre-pruning), or grow a tree then remove weak branches (post-pruning). Ensembles such as random forests combine many trees for often stronger predictions, at the cost of a less direct explanation.
